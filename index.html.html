Practice: the live 911 call

Sam the dispatcher talks kids through a pretend emergency, following real dispatch order: location first, then name, callback number, details, safety, and instructions. Free practice, or pick from 15 scenarios: fires, medical emergencies (choking, seizure, pills, bee sting, drowning), police (stranger at the door, being followed, lost at the fair), a car crash, and two "should I even call 911?" cases.

  • How Sam talks. He asks for the address once, then helps the child find one (mail, a fridge magnet, Alexa or Google Home). He rewords a question instead of repeating it, gives real answers to "what's your name?" and "are you coming?", takes self-harm talk seriously, and for prank calls he teaches kindly instead of scolding.
  • Scoring. Each call gets a score out of 100, kid-friendly badges (Truth Teller, Emergency Expert, Line Saver and others), a plain-language summary, a safety tip, and a read-aloud option. Calls scoring 75 or higher offer a printable certificate.
  • How it runs. Apple on-device speech recognition keeps the first moments of audio so first words are never lost. Three Core ML models plus keyword rules understand the child, a small built-in AI (Qwen 1.5B) helps read what was said, and an optional second model (Anthropic) is used only if a key is entered. Sam's wording is scripted by a hand-written brain. Voice is Kokoro on the phone, with Apple's voice as fallback. Azure and ElevenLabs voices are optional.

Learn

Six lessons: Calling 911, Know Your Address, Fire Safety, Someone Is Hurt, Someone Is In Danger, and When NOT to Call 911. Lessons are read aloud in a dispatcher voice with the line highlighted. Each has a Do's and Don'ts view and a quiz. Kids earn stars and trophies, fill a Trophy Case, and win the "Safety Champion" crown for earning every trophy, with confetti and sound effects. Printable certificate via AirPrint or PDF.

Safety Town: the 2D game

Make a character (boy or girl, skin, hair color and style, 7 outfits, a name) and choose a house color and address. Then walk a flat 2D cartoon town with a joystick: your house, school, a friend's house, a park, a shop, fire and police stations, roads with traffic, and townspeople. Inside your house is a cut-away view with four rooms, where events can happen too.

  • Stories. 16 scenarios with three choices each, covering stranger danger, fires, medical problems, a crash, getting lost, and prank dares. A wrong answer explains why and lets you try again.
  • 911 calls in the game. When the right choice is "call 911", you dial the real keypad and talk to Sam. After a good call, a siren plays and police, fire, or ambulance vehicles drive to you.
  • Rewards and saving. Stars (3 on the first try) and badges. The game autosaves and has a day-and-night clock.
  • In progress. Talking to people in town, and seeing your character brush their teeth or play, are on the way.

For Grown-Ups (Face ID lock)

A locked area for parents and guardians. Child profiles, parent profiles, your local police contact, dispatcher settings, and a missing-child report builder, all behind Face ID.

  • Child profiles. A current photo with a 6-month refresh reminder (also after a haircut, glasses, or braces), plus a photo history so police can see how your child has changed. Fields cover name and nickname, birth date, height and weight with a "measured today" button, appearance (hair color and style, eye color, glasses, braces, birthmarks, scars, freckles, piercings), health needs (medical conditions, allergies, medications, communication or special needs), school, friends and places, phone and online accounts (including chat usernames), and other information for police (custody arrangements, where the child ID kit is kept).
  • Parent profiles and your police department. Parent contact and home address, plus a field for your local police contact, so everything is in one place when seconds count.
  • "My child is missing". A calm step-by-step flow: 1. Call 911 right now, with the reminder that there is no waiting period and the first hours matter most, plus what to do while you wait (check closets, under beds, cars; ask neighbors and friends). 2. Who is missing, pulled from the child profile. 3. When and where they were last seen, what they were wearing, who they were with, any vehicle. 4. Create the report: a printable PDF with your child's photo, description, last-seen details, and your contact information, ready to hand to officers. It also lists the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (1-800-THE-LOST, open 24 hours) and missingkids.org. The app never sends anything on its own, and it states clearly that it does not replace calling 911.
  • Settings. Dispatcher voice picker with a preview, built-in natural voice toggle, free on-device Apple premium voices, optional Azure or ElevenLabs keys, built-in AI toggle, and a Face ID lock switch.

Privacy

911 Kids is built to keep your family private.

  • Everything runs on the device: speech, understanding, the AI, and the voice. No account or key is needed.
  • Profiles and photos stay on the phone. Family profiles are stored only on the device and are never uploaded. They are shared only when you choose to send a report.
  • Practice calls are pretend. They never contact real emergency services. The missing-child report is created by you and sent only where you choose to send it.
  • Optional cloud features stay off unless you turn them on. Azure or ElevenLabs voices and the Anthropic conversation model are used only if you enter a key.
  • Why the app is large. The built-in models (Qwen plus Kokoro) make the download big, but the game itself is drawn in code and adds almost nothing.
  • No ads, no tracking, no analytics. The app does not collect or sell data.

Does the app place real 911 calls?

No. Every call in 911 Kids is pretend practice. The app never dials real emergency services. The Dial 911 button opens a practice keypad, and the practice call screen is clearly a simulation with Sam, the practice dispatcher.

Is my child's voice or photo uploaded anywhere?

No. Speech recognition, understanding, the AI, and the voice all run on the device. Profiles and photos stay on the phone and are shared only when you choose to email or share a report you created.

What does the Face ID lock do?

It locks the For Grown-Ups area so only a parent or guardian can open child profiles, the missing-child report builder, and settings.

Why is the download large?

The built-in AI models (Qwen 1.5B for understanding and Kokoro for the dispatcher voice) run entirely on the phone so nothing needs the cloud. The game itself is drawn in code and adds almost nothing to the size.

Which devices are supported?

911 Kids is built for iPhone and iPad.
